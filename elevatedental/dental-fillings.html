Stage 01, plain English

What a composite filling is

Decay is a hole, and a hole does not heal.

Once bacteria have dissolved through the enamel into the softer dentine underneath, the cavity keeps widening until the damaged tissue is cut away and the space is sealed. That is what a filling does: it removes the decay and restores the missing structure so the tooth can be loaded again.

We restore with composite resin, a tooth-coloured material made of a resin matrix loaded with fine glass or ceramic particles. It is placed soft, shaped directly in the cavity, and set hard with a curing light in layers of about two millimetres at a time. Because it bonds chemically to the prepared enamel and dentine, less healthy tooth has to be cut away to hold it than an older mechanical restoration would require.

Most of these are completed in one appointment.

The step people miss

Shade is matched to your tooth before the cavity is opened, against a shade guide and in natural light where possible. On a front tooth, more than one shade is often layered so the restoration follows the tooth's own translucency rather than sitting on it as a flat patch. A tooth dries out under isolation and lightens while it does, which is exactly why the decision is taken first rather than at the end.

Stage 02

When a filling is the right repair, and when it is not

A composite restoration is correct when decay has broken through the enamel but the remaining tooth is still strong enough to support it.

It also repairs a chipped edge, a worn notch at the gum line, and small cosmetic defects at the front of the mouth. If one of the four turnings below describes your tooth, take it, and the treatment named on it is the one to read next.

Turning 01 Too little tooth

A crown or an onlay

When the cavity is large, the tooth has lost a cusp, or the remaining walls are thin, a filling is being asked to do a crown's job. It will flex under load and the tooth will eventually split. We will say which restoration is correct before we start rather than after the first one fails.

Dental crowns
Turning 02 Nerve involved

Root canal treatment first

When decay has reached the pulp, the nerve is already infected, and sealing a restoration over an infected nerve traps the problem. Persistent throbbing, pain that wakes you at night, or lingering sensitivity to heat are the signs, and imaging confirms it.

Root canal treatment
Turning 03 Cracked, not decayed

A crack is a different problem

When a tooth is cracked rather than decayed, a restoration placed inside it can hold the crack open under bite pressure. Cracked teeth are assessed on their own terms, and the assessment decides the treatment.

General dentistry
Turning 04 Cosmetic only

Direct veneers for shape

Where the tooth is sound and what you want changed is its shape, its edge or its shade rather than a cavity in it, the same material is used differently. That treatment is a direct veneer, and it is planned against your face rather than against a cavity outline.

Direct veneers

The case for doing nothing yet

Very early decay that has not yet broken the enamel surface does not need cutting at all. It can often be arrested with fluoride and a change of routine, and we will monitor it rather than drill it. A tooth that has not been opened is still a tooth that has never been opened.

Stage 03, image to polish

How the appointment runs

Six steps, in this order, with the time each one takes at the chair.

A single-surface restoration is typically 30 to 45 minutes in the chair. A larger multi-surface one, or several teeth in one sitting, runs longer.

  1. Consultation and diagnosis, 10 to 15 minutes. Examination, and imaging where the cavity sits between teeth or close to the nerve. You are shown what is on the image, on a screen, during the appointment rather than afterwards.
  2. Anesthetic, 5 to 10 minutes including onset. Given where the cavity is deep or close to the nerve. Small, shallow cavities are often restored without it, and that is your choice, not an assumption.
  3. Decay removal, 5 to 15 minutes. All softened tissue is removed and the cavity is shaped to hold the restoration. Isolation keeps the field dry, which is what makes the bond hold.
  4. Bonding and layering, 10 to 20 minutes. A bonding agent is applied, then composite is placed and light-cured in layers, each shaped to the tooth's own anatomy rather than flattened.
  5. Bite check and adjustment, about 5 minutes. Occlusion is checked and adjusted. A restoration that sits even slightly high will ache under load, so this step is not optional.
  6. Finishing and polish, about 5 minutes. The surface is contoured and polished so it resists staining and does not hold plaque at the margin.

Stage 04, the bench and its instruments

Who performs it, and what they decide from

Dental fillings are performed by our General Dentistry specialists.

Where the assessment shows that the nerve is involved, the case moves to our endodontics specialists, and where the tooth needs full coverage it moves to prosthodontics. Both sit in the same group, so the case is handled in house instead of being referred to another practice mid-treatment.

Every clinician who performs this treatment is listed by name, discipline and training on our specialists page.

We name the systems because a name is checkable. An adjective is not.

What moves the final figure

  • How many surfaces of the tooth are involved, and how large the cavity is.
  • Whether the tooth is a front tooth needing layered shade work, or a back tooth carrying chewing load.
  • How many teeth are restored in the same appointment.
  • Whether local anesthetic and imaging are required.
  • Whether the assessment calls for an onlay, a crown or root canal treatment instead, each of which is priced separately.

Your exact fee is confirmed at consultation, before treatment begins.

What the figure buys

A well-placed composite restoration matches the surrounding enamel closely enough that most people cannot identify which tooth was treated, returns full chewing function, and seals the tooth against further decay at that site. These are typically expected to serve for several years and are then repaired or replaced as the margin wears. How long yours lasts depends on the size of the cavity, where it sits in the arch, your bite forces and your home care.

A restoration is a repair, not a replacement for the original tooth. Decay can start again at a new site, which is why the six-month recall matters more afterwards, not less.

Questions, aftercare, and the consultation

A first appointment produces three things: the tooth imaged and assessed, a plain answer about which restoration it needs, and a fee in writing. None of them commits you to treatment on the day.

Before you agree to it

Will it hurt?

Local anesthetic is used wherever the cavity is deep or near the nerve, so the preparation itself is not felt. You may feel vibration and water. Small shallow cavities are often restored comfortably without anesthetic, and that decision is yours.

How long does one last?

Several years is the usual expectation, and small restorations in low-load positions tend to serve longer than large ones on molars. Bite force, grinding, and how well the margin is kept clean all shorten or extend it. Your dentist checks every existing restoration at each recall.

Can I choose a silver one instead?

We restore with tooth-coloured composite. If you have an existing amalgam restoration that is sound, it does not need replacing simply because it is visible. If it is failing, we will show you the margin and explain the options.

Do I need an image first?

Usually yes, where the cavity is between teeth or under an existing restoration, because those surfaces cannot be assessed by eye. You are shown what it shows and told what it means.

Afterwards, and the days that follow

Can I eat straight afterwards?

Composite is fully set by the time you leave, so yes. If you have had local anesthetic, wait until the numbness has gone before eating, because a numb lip and cheek are easy to bite.

My tooth is sensitive. Is that normal?

Short-lived sensitivity to cold and to pressure is common for a few days and sometimes a few weeks after a deep restoration, as the nerve settles. What is not normal is pain that builds, wakes you at night, or lingers after heat. Call us if that happens.

My bite feels high.

Come back. It is a two-minute adjustment. A restoration a fraction of a millimetre proud will make the tooth ache under load and can crack it, and it does not settle on its own.

What should the next 24 hours look like?

Wait for numbness to pass before eating. For the first 24 hours, chew on the other side if the restoration is large, and avoid biting directly into very hard food such as ice or bone with that tooth. Brush and floss it normally from the same day: the margin between material and tooth is where new decay starts, so it needs cleaning, not protecting.
